## The Problem

Peer-to-peer car rental sounds simple — list your car, someone rents it — but the trust and logistics problems are significant. The owner needs to know who's driving their car. The renter needs to know their payment is protected. And both sides need a booking system that won't double-book the same car on overlapping dates.

Existing solutions are either too simple (a Google Sheet and bank transfer) or too complex (enterprise fleet management software). There was no middle ground for a small marketplace that needed real payments, real verification, and real availability management.

## The Approach

I built MoRent as a full-stack Next.js 14 application with Prisma and PostgreSQL. The core is a two-sided marketplace: owners list vehicles, renters browse and book, and a shared booking engine manages availability.

For payments, I used Stripe Connect. The renter's payment is held in escrow — charged at booking, released to the owner after the trip is confirmed complete. This protects both sides: the renter isn't charged until the booking is confirmed, and the owner doesn't release the car without payment secured.

For identity verification, I built a document-upload flow where renters verify their license before booking. Owners see verified status on renter profiles. It's not a full KYC system, but it raises the trust floor significantly compared to an anonymous booking.

Availability checking is done at the database level with overlapping-date queries — the booking engine checks for date conflicts atomically, so two renters can't book the same car for overlapping periods.

The project was built as part of CodeMigos cohort 6, deployed to Vercel with a live database.

## The Result

The MVP is deployed and functional: owners can list cars, renters can browse, verify identity, book with Stripe payments, and complete trips with escrow release. The booking engine handles date-conflict prevention at the database level.

The full stack runs on Next.js 14, Prisma, PostgreSQL, Stripe Connect, and NextAuth — a production-grade stack for a marketplace that needs to handle real money and real trust.

## Context

MoRent was built as part of CodeMigos cohort 6, a six-week intensive where the goal was a working, deployed product rather than a prototype. The brief was deliberately open: pick a domain with real transactional complexity and ship it end to end.

I chose a two-sided marketplace because it forces every hard backend problem into one project — authentication with multiple roles, payments with escrow semantics, relational data with real constraints, and a booking engine where correctness matters. A CRUD app wouldn't have exercised those muscles.

The trust problem framed the whole design. In peer-to-peer rental, neither side knows the other. That means identity verification isn't a nice-to-have, payments can't be simple one-shot charges, and availability has to be enforced at the database level rather than trusted to the client.

## Decisions & Trade-offs

**Stripe Connect over manual payouts.** I considered collecting payments and paying owners manually via bank transfer. Stripe Connect's escrow model (charge on booking, release after trip confirmation) protects both parties automatically and removes me from the flow of money. Trade-off: more integration complexity and Stripe's fees, but the trust guarantees are worth it for a marketplace.

**Database-level overlap checking over application-level locking.** Checking for date conflicts in application code and then writing the booking leaves a race window between check and write. I used overlapping-date queries that run atomically in the database, so two concurrent bookings can't both succeed. Trade-off: a slightly more complex query, but correctness under concurrency.

**Document-upload verification over full KYC.** A real KYC provider (document scan + face match) was out of scope for six weeks. I built a license-upload flow with manual review status — it raises the trust floor without the integration overhead. Trade-off: not automated, not production-grade, but demonstrable and honest about what it is.

## Reflection

The hardest part wasn't any single integration — it was making the booking engine correct. Double-booking is the failure mode that kills a rental marketplace, and the naive implementation (check, then insert) is subtly wrong under concurrency. Testing that path with simulated concurrent requests was the moment the project felt solid.

If I extended it, the next steps would be automated identity verification, a review system after trips, and inventory pricing rules (weekend vs weekday). The escrow model would also need a dispute flow — right now release is triggered by trip completion with no conflict path.
